ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · PARIS

Daft Punk

Two Parisians in robot helmets taught the whole planet to dance in French — then vanished at the height of their powers.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · OCTOBER 17, 2025 · 2 MIN READ

Guy-Manuel de Homem-Christo and Thomas Bangalter met as teenagers at Paris's Lycée Carnot, played in a guitar band a British critic called "daft punky thrash," and took the insult as a band name. In 1993 they started making house music in a bedroom in Paris. Within four years, that bedroom had annexed the world.

The French touch — la French touch — was Paris's answer to everything: filter-swept disco samples, compressed drums, a Gallic shrug aimed at Anglo house music. Homework (1997) put it on the map with "Around the World" and "Da Funk." Then came the helmets, and with them, the great disappearing act of pop history: two men who became the most recognizable faces in electronic music by refusing to show their faces at all.

Discovery (2001) was the masterpiece — a neon anime odyssey with videos by Leiji Matsumoto, the creator of Interstella 5555, that turned an album into a Saturday-morning cartoon for the club generation. The 2006 Coachella pyramid set rewired live electronic music; every LED cube on every festival main stage since is a footnote to it. Random Access Memories (2013) went the other way — live musicians, disco maximalism, "Get Lucky" — and swept the Grammys, Album of the Year included.

Paris runs through all of it: Bangalter's Roulé label, the Crydamoure side projects, the whole Ed Banger generation (Justice chief among them) that inherited the throne. Daft Punk made Paris the capital of electronic cool for two decades, and they did it while barely giving an interview.

Then, on February 22, 2021, they posted an eight-minute video called "Epilogue" — a clip from their 2006 film Electroma — and walked off into the desert. No reunion tour, no farewell interviews, no reunion tour merch drop. The robots simply powered down. In an era when every legacy act is on its third comeback, the discipline of that exit might be their most punk move of all.

The key track

"One More Time" (2000). The Discovery opener that bottled euphoria itself — a chopped-up Romanthony vocal riding a filter that opens like sunrise. Play it anywhere on earth and the room agrees on everything for three minutes. That's not a song, that's diplomacy.

Start here

Discovery (2001). Fourteen tracks, zero skips, and the anime film Interstella 5555 if you want the full visual trip. Then work backward to Homework for the raw Paris basement energy, and forward to Random Access Memories for the victory lap.

The take: Daft Punk proved you could be the biggest band on the planet while being, technically, nobody. Paris gave them the mystique; the helmets gave them immortality. The robots retired. The pyramid remains.
