ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · NASHVILLE

Dolly Parton: Nashville's patron saint

She arrived in Nashville the day after high school graduation with a guitar and a plan. The city never stood a chance.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · DECEMBER 11, 2025 · 4 MIN READ

Dolly Parton moved to Nashville in 1964, the morning after she graduated high school in Sevier County, Tennessee. Sixty years later, she is the closest thing Music City has to a patron saint — a songwriter so good she wrote "Jolene" and "I Will Always Love You" in the same day, on the same session, and then gave the second one to Whitney Houston's eternity.

The business story is the real Nashville story. Parton played the long game on Music Row: years as Porter Wagoner's duet partner on his syndicated TV show, then a ruthless, brilliant solo breakout. She owned her publishing when women in country were expected to smile and cash the checks men wrote. "9 to 5" wasn't just a hit — it was a labor anthem from someone who understood the grind because she never left it.

Why Nashville claims her

Parton's Nashville isn't the honky-tonk tourist strip — it's the songwriting economy. She built her career in the rooms where the Bluebird Cafe ethos was born: songs first, everything else second. The Grand Ole Opry made her a member; she made the Opry feel like it had to live up to her. And the Imagination Library — her free-book program that has mailed hundreds of millions of books to children — is headquartered in Tennessee, because of course it is.

Local lore: songwriters on Music Row still talk about her work ethic the way athletes talk about training camp. Show up, write, outwork everyone, be kind anyway.

Dolly didn't conquer Nashville by playing its game. She bought the board, rewrote the rules, and donated the proceeds.

Then she built a theme park. Dollywood, in the Smoky Mountains near her hometown, opened in 1986 and became one of America's most beloved attractions — a monument to the idea that a girl from Sevier County could build anything she imagined. Between the park, the publishing empire, and the books, Parton's business résumé reads like a case study they should teach at Vanderbilt.

Key track

"Jolene" — two minutes and forty-one seconds of pleading so intense it launched a thousand response songs, a Beyoncé cover, and one of the great mysteries of country music: who was Jolene, really? Dolly's never fully said.

Start here

Play Jolene (the 1974 original) back-to-back with "I Will Always Love You" — same day, same pen. Then watch her 2019 MusiCares tribute at the Grammys to see what Nashville thinks of her. Bring tissues.

Nashville has a lot of stars. It has exactly one Dolly, and the city knows what it has.
