Trombone Shorty: Treme's brass-band ambassador

Troy Andrews was leading brass bands at six. Now he's the man who carries New Orleans' second-line tradition to the world.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · APRIL 16, 2026 · 4 MIN READ

The nickname came first: "Trombone Shorty," because Troy Andrews was a small kid from the Tremé carrying a trombone nearly as big as he was. He started playing in brass bands at six years old — six — parading through New Orleans streets before he could ride a bike without training wheels. By his teens he was touring; by his twenties he was the tradition's global ambassador.

Shorty's genius is translation. Albums like Backatown (2010) and Say That to Say This (2013) take the second-line brass-band vocabulary — the parade rhythms, the call-and-response, the sheer joyful noise — and wire it into funk, rock, and hip-hop without losing a drop of the street. He's played with everyone from Lenny Kravitz to the Foo Fighters to Macklemore, and every collaboration sounds like New Orleans winning the argument.

Why New Orleans claims him

In a city where the brass band is a civic institution, Shorty is the institution's favorite son. He closes out the New Orleans Jazz & Heritage Festival — the coveted final set, the hometown coronation — and his annual "Shorty Fest" benefits the Trombone Shorty Foundation, which puts instruments in the hands of New Orleans kids. That's the whole tradition in one gesture: the parade never stops, it just gets younger.

Local lore: he played his first Jazz Fest as a child, and the festival grew up around him. Ask anyone in the Tremé — they'll tell you they knew.

New Orleans doesn't export its culture; it parades it. Trombone Shorty is the parade's loudest, brightest banner.

He's also the tradition's most visible teacher. His appearances on HBO's Treme introduced the brass-band world to a global audience, and the Trombone Shorty Foundation's work — instruments, instruction, and the annual Shorty Fest — keeps the pipeline full. Every kid who picks up a horn in the Tremé now has a hometown hero to point at. That's how a century-old tradition makes it to century two.

Key track

"Hurricane Season" — the Backatown burner that bottles second-line energy into four minutes. Play it at volume and try to sit still. You can't.

Start here

Backatown for the mission statement, then catch him live — preferably closing Jazz Fest, where the whole city shows up to claim him one more time.

The trombone was bigger than the kid. Now the kid is bigger than the tradition — and the tradition is bigger for it.
