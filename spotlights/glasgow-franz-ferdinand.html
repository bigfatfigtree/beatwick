ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · GLASGOW

Franz Ferdinand

Four art-school misfits who turned Glasgow's post-punk attic into the biggest guitar band of the 2000s.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · DECEMBER 2, 2025 · 2 MIN READ

Glasgow in 2002 had Mogwai's noise, Belle and Sebastian's whisper, and a rehearsal space called The Chateau where Alex Kapranos, Nick McCarthy, Bob Hardy, and Paul Thomson were cooking up something else entirely: Gang of Four guitars, disco drums, and choruses with the confidence of a band that had already decided it was famous. Their self-titled debut arrived in 2004 and made the decision for them.

Franz Ferdinand was the sound of the post-punk revival done right — sharp suits, sharper riffs, and "Take Me Out," a song with two intros and a gear change that detonated on every dancefloor on earth. The album won the Mercury Prize, went to number three in the UK and number twelve in the US, and earned two Grammy nominations. For a few years, the biggest guitar band in the world rehearsed in a Glasgow warehouse.

What set them apart from the landfill-indie hordes was the art-school brain underneath the suits. Kapranos wrote like a man who'd read the books — literary references, Russian-constructivist sleeve art, a genuine interest in making pop music smart. Glasgow has always produced bands with ideas; Franz Ferdinand were the ones who made ideas dance.

The Glasgow lore runs deep: Domino Records took the chance, the Chateau days became legend, and the band became the city's most reliable export since the shipyards — proof that Glasgow's conveyor belt of great guitar bands (Orange Juice, The Jesus and Mary Chain, Primal Scream) had another generation in it. They've never really gone away: You Could Have It So Much Better, Tonight, the FFS collaboration with Sparks, and a live show that still hits like 2004.

The key track

"Take Me Out" (2004). The false-start intro, the gear change at 1:20, the riff that ate the decade. It's the rare song that works as well in a stadium as it does as a ringtone — which is to say, it's perfect pop smuggled inside a post-punk song.

Start here

Franz Ferdinand (2004), front to back — twelve tracks, no filler, the sound of a band that had been rehearsing for this exact moment. Then "Do You Want To" for the second album's sugar rush, and the FFS record with Sparks for proof they never lost their nerve.

The take: Franz Ferdinand took Glasgow's art-punk attic and turned it into the decade's dancefloor. The suits were a statement: guitar music could be clever, sexy, and fun at the same time. It still can.
