ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · GLASGOW

Belle and Sebastian

The shyest band in Glasgow became its most beloved — twee as a weapon, melodies as armor.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · JUNE 25, 2026 · 2 MIN READ

Belle and Sebastian formed in Glasgow in 1996, assembled by Stuart Murdoch for what was essentially a college music-course project at Stow College. The plan was one album. The result was Tigermilk — pressed in a run of a thousand copies — and then, months later, If You're Feeling Sinister, one of the great indie records ever made. The band refused press photos, gave cryptic interviews, and let the mystery do the marketing. It worked embarrassingly well.

The sound is the opposite of everything Glasgow was famous for: no noise, no swagger, just Murdoch's bookish melodies, chamber-pop arrangements, and lyrics about the beautiful losers of student life. "The Boy with the Arab Strap," "Get Me Away from Here, I'm Dying," "Like Dylan in the Movies" — songs that treat shyness as a superpower and heartbreak as literature. The twee label was meant as an insult; the band wore it like a medal.

Glasgow adopted them the way Glasgow adopts everything: completely. The early anonymity became legend — fans trading Tigermilk originals like relics — and the band grew up in public, from the Jeepster years to the polished Dear Catastrophe Waitress (2004) and beyond. Their Barrowland Christmas shows became a city institution: the loudest room in Glasgow, gone quiet for the quietest band.

Three decades on, they're still going — still Murdoch's songs, still the collective spirit, still releasing records that sound like nobody else. In a city that produces guitar bands like weather, Belle and Sebastian are the permanent climate: gentle, literate, and utterly unkillable.

The key track

"Get Me Away from Here, I'm Dying" (1998). The title is the whole band in seven words — a plea for escape set to the prettiest melody they ever wrote. It's funny, it's sad, and it understands you better than your friends do.

Start here

If You're Feeling Sinister (1996) — the masterpiece, the one that made the legend. Then The Boy with the Arab Strap (1998) for the fuller-band version, and Dear Catastrophe Waitress (2004) for the polished pop peak.

The take: Belle and Sebastian proved Glasgow's softest band could be its toughest — three decades of melodies that outlasted every scene that tried to dismiss them. Shyness, weaponized.
