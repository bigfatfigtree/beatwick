ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · BOGOTÁ

LAS AÑEZ

Twin sisters from Bogotá, two loop stations, and Colombian folk bent into impossible shapes — Las Añez have award-winning albums, Andrea Echeverri features, and harmonies from another dimension.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · APRIL 8, 2026 · 3 MIN READ

Listen → Listen on Spotify

Start with “De Curvo Cuerpo” — performed on the KEXP full session — twin harmonies doing things physics barely allows.

Valentina and Juanita Áñez Rothmann perform with almost nothing: two voices, a loop station, some minimal percussion, a small keyboard. What comes out is maximal — Colombian folk traditions like joropo and Andean rhythms reimagined as surreal, enchanting atmospheres, built in real time and improvised on stage with calculated perfection. Nobody else in Latin music sounds like the sisters from Bogotá.

The catalogue is deep and decorated. Silbidos (2017), Al Aire (2017) — featuring a collaboration with Aterciopelados icon Andrea Echeverri — Reflexión (2020), and Paralelas (2023). A best-album nod from Colombia's Ministry of Culture, composition awards from Ibermúsicas and IDARTES, and "De Curvo Cuerpo" named Radiónica's most important song of 2022. This is not underground-by-default; it's underground by choice.

WHY BOGOTÁ CLAIMS THEM

Bogotá's IDARTES composition awards found them first, back in 2013 — the city recognized the experiment before the world did. The sisters blend folklore with contemporary music the way Bogotá itself blends the Andes with the modern capital, and their music has become one of the city's most exportable ideas.

THE KEY TRACK

"De Curvo Cuerpo." The Radiónica-anointed one — twin harmonies doing things physics barely allows, folk melody bent into a shape that sounds like the future. NPR's Alt.Latino came calling too, featuring "Cebolla" and "Libéralo" on its new-music roundups.

THE TRAJECTORY

WOMEX showcases, tours across Latin America and Europe, film and TV placements — their music scored the series One Hundred Years of Solitude — and a US debut in New York. The sisters who improvise on Bogotá stages with loop stations and a cuatro are now being heard on screens worldwide, and the songs never stopped being weird. That's the whole point.

What keeps them in rotation is how little they repeat themselves. From the jazz-leaning Al Aire to the folk-forward Paralelas and their 2025 soundtrack work, every album rethinks what two voices and a loop station can do. And the twin-harmony alchemy is genuinely singular — siblings who've sung together their whole lives produce intervals and lockstep phrasing that can't be learned, only inherited. Audiences in Latin America and Europe have been charmed by it for years; the rest of the world is just catching up.

START HERE

"De Curvo Cuerpo," then "Dados al Aire" with Andrea Echeverri, then the Al Aire and Paralelas albums in order. Headphones mandatory.

Las Añez didn't modernize Colombian folk. They proved it was futuristic all along.
