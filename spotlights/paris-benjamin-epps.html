ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · PARIS

BENJAMIN EPPS

The Gabonese-born Paris MC keeping boom bap alive with a Jadakiss intro, a BET Hip Hop Award, and the sharpest French-language pen of his generation.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · FEBRUARY 11, 2026 · 3 MIN READ

Benjamin Epps raps like it's 1995 and he's got something to prove about it. Born Kesstate Epembia in Libreville, Gabon, he grew up battle-rapping on local radio, moved to France in 2015, and taught himself to produce, record and mix his own music — all while building one of the most technically ferocious flows in francophone rap. The Philharmonie de Paris booked him as the révélation of the nouvelle garde hip-hop, and the BET Hip Hop Awards gave him Best International Flow in 2022. His heroes are Nas, Biggie and Lino. His voice is high, nasal, unmistakable, and it cuts through dusty soul samples like a blade.

The discography tells the story of a self-made MC: EPs Le Futur (2020), Fantôme avec chauffeur (2021) and Vous N'Êtes Pas Contents ? Triplé (2022) — the last featuring an intro from Jadakiss himself and a JeanJass-produced banger — then the debut album La Grande Désillusion (2023) on the indie Mocabe Nation, stacked with Styles P, MC Solaar, Josman, Angélique Kidjo and Lino. He followed with the solo mixtape Ready for War and, in 2025, L'Enfant Sacré de Bellevue with Conway the Machine. He even has an Olympia headline under his belt.

WHY PARIS CLAIMS HIM

Epps built his French career the Parisian way: small rooms, self-released maxis, co-signs from Dinos and Youssoupha, and a 2022 Philharmonie de Paris showcase that framed him as the new guard's standard-bearer. In a city where drill dominates the charts, he's the one MC proving the boom bap tradition never died — it just moved to Gabon and back.

THE KEY TRACK

“Kennedy en 2005” (2020). The standout from Le Futur: a dense, acerbic flow over stripped-back boom bap, the track that made purists and casuals agree this guy could genuinely rap. For the full ego-trip mode, “Drillmatic” — introduced by Jadakiss, no less.

THE VOICE

High-pitched, nasal, relentless. Epps packs bars with internal rhymes and punchlines the way his New York idols did, but the French diction gives it a different music — percussive, rolling, impossibly dense. Comparisons to Westside Gunn follow him around; they share influences more than a sound, and Epps' pen is the argument-ender either way.

START HERE

La Grande Désillusion for the full canvas, then the Triplé EP for the hungriest version of him. Then find footage of that Philharmonie set and watch a whole room convert.

Benjamin Epps didn't revive boom bap. He just never let it die.
