ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · SÃO PAULO

Rita Lee

The queen of Brazilian rock — São Paulo's own, from Tropicália to eternity.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · APRIL 14, 2026 · 2 MIN READ

Rita Lee Jones was born on December 31, 1947, in São Paulo, and at eighteen she formed Os Mutantes with Arnaldo Baptista and Sérgio Dias — the psychedelic trio that became the house band of Tropicália, making fuzzed-out, carnivalesque rock under a military dictatorship. Kurt Cobain tracked them down in Brazil in 1993 to tell them their music was revolutionary. She left the band in 1972 and got even bigger.

The solo career, built with her band Tutti Frutti and her husband and partner Roberto de Carvalho, made her the queen of Brazilian rock: Fruto Proibido (1975), "Lança Perfume" (1980), "Mania de Você," and a catalog of more than forty records spanning six decades. She won a Latin Grammy for 3001 in 2001 and never stopped being gloriously, defiantly herself.

São Paulo claimed her completely. Caetano Veloso's song "Sampa" named her the most complete definition of the city — the red hair, the irreverence, the rock-and-roll attitude as civic identity. She died in May 2023, in her São Paulo home, and the city mourned her like family. Because she was.

Her 2016 autobiography was a bestseller — funny, filthy, and honest in exactly the way you'd expect from her. She spent her final years as São Paulo's beloved elder stateswoman of rock, the rare legend who got to hear the eulogies while she was still around to enjoy them. The city threw her a farewell worthy of royalty.

She was still recording, still provocative, still unmistakably herself into her seventies — a rock star who aged without ever softening. São Paulo doesn't produce many saints. It produced exactly one, and she played guitar.

The key track

"Ovelha Negra," from Fruto Proibido (1975). The black-sheep ballad — a woman declaring herself the family's beautiful disgrace over one of the great melodies in Brazilian rock. It's the defining statement of the country's first rock queen, and it still sounds like freedom.

Start here

"Ovelha Negra" first, then the whole Fruto Proibido album — it's her Exile on Main St., minus the exile. Then "Lança Perfume" for the pop genius, and one Mutantes record — the 1968 debut — to hear where the legend started.

Fifty years of rock stardom, zero compromises, one city that never stopped claiming her. Saint Rita of Sampa, indeed.
