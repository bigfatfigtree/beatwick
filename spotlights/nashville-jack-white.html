ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · NASHVILLE

Jack White: Nashville's rock-and-roll contrarian

The Detroit garage-rocker who moved his whole operation to Nashville and built the city's loudest independent institution.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · MARCH 19, 2026 · 4 MIN READ

Jack White was born in Detroit and became famous as half of the White Stripes — two people, three colors, one of the great guitar riffs ever recorded. Then he did the thing nobody expected: he moved his entire operation to Nashville and built Third Man Records into the most gloriously stubborn institution in modern music.

Third Man's Nashville headquarters is part label, part record store, part vinyl pressing plant, part live venue (the Blue Room), and part museum of analog obsession. In an era when the industry went fully digital, White built a temple to tape machines and lathe cuts — and made it profitable. The White Stripes' "Seven Nation Army" riff is now chanted in football stadiums on every continent, which has to be the strangest second act for a garage-rock duo in history.

Why Nashville claims him

White chose Nashville precisely because it wasn't the obvious choice — and then became one of its defining citizens. Third Man anchors a whole alternative economy on the city's west side: indie bands, pressing-plant jobs, a record-store culture that treats vinyl like religion. He's collaborated with Loretta Lynn and produced her late-career comeback Van Lear Rose, bridging the garage and the Grand Ole Opry in a way only Nashville could accommodate.

Local lore: Third Man's Blue Room shows are announced with almost no notice and sell out instantly — the city's worst-kept best secret.

Every music city has a contrarian. Nashville's bought a building, installed a record press, and dares the industry to keep up.

The side quests are just as telling. The Raconteurs and the Dead Weather — his other bands — let him play sideman, drummer, and frontman in rotation, and Third Man expanded to a second outpost in Detroit, closing the circle on his hometown. White treats rock and roll like a trade to be apprenticed in, and Nashville is where he built the workshop.

Key track

"Seven Nation Army" — the riff that ate the world. Seven notes, one guitar, and a stadium chant that will outlive us all.

Start here

Elephant (2003) for the Stripes at their peak, then Blunderbuss (2012) for the solo-era range. If you're in Nashville, the Third Man storefront is a pilgrimage — go for the live direct-to-acetate recordings.

Detroit made Jack White. Nashville gave him a headquarters — and he gave it back a whole new reason to be loud.
