ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · BUENOS AIRES

Soda Stereo

The trio that made Buenos Aires the capital of Spanish-language rock.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · DECEMBER 17, 2025 · 2 MIN READ

In 1982, three university kids from Buenos Aires — Gustavo Cerati on guitar and vocals, Zeta Bosio on bass, Charly Alberti on drums — started a band with new-wave haircuts and stadium-sized ambition. Within a decade, Soda Stereo was the biggest rock band in the Spanish-speaking world, and Buenos Aires was its undisputed capital.

The run is absurd: Nada Personal (1985), Signos (1986), Doble Vida (1988, cut in New York with David Bowie's guitarist Carlos Alomar), the masterpiece Canción Animal (1990), the shoegaze detour Dynamo (1992), and Sueño Stereo (1995). They toured the continent like no Argentine band before them, playing to hundreds of thousands from Mexico to Chile.

The city's rooms are part of the legend. Estadio Obras Sanitarias — "El Templo del Rock" — consecrated them. The farewell, the Último Concierto, filled River Plate's Monumental stadium in September 1997; the reunion tour a decade later, Me Verás Volver, did it again. When Cerati died in 2014, after four years in a coma, the country mourned like it had lost a head of state.

The 2007 reunion tour, Me Verás Volver, was a victory lap across the continent — proof that no band ever owned Latin America the way they did. And Cerati's solo records, especially Bocanada (1999) and Ahí Vamos (2006), are essential listening in their own right: the same genius, quieter rooms, zero drop in ambition.

The key track

"De Música Ligera," the closing track of Canción Animal. Four chords, one riff, and the most famous guitar figure in Latin rock — the song every Argentine knows by heart, and the last song of that final River Plate show. It sounds simple. It took a genius to make it sound simple.

Start here

"De Música Ligera" on the biggest speakers you own. Then Canción Animal front to back, then "En la Ciudad de la Furia" to understand why the city's nickname for itself fits. Cerati's solo records — Ahí Vamos, Fuerza Natural — are the graduate course.

Every rock capital needs a band that proves the local language can fill a stadium. Buenos Aires had the best one.
