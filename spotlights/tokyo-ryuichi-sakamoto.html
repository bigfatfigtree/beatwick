ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · TOKYO

Ryuichi Sakamoto

Tokyo's most restless genius — pop star, film composer, activist — never stopped remaking the city's sound.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · NOVEMBER 5, 2025 · 3 MIN READ

Ryuichi Sakamoto was born in Tokyo in 1952 and spent the next seventy years refusing to stay in any lane the city offered him. Synth-pop pioneer, Oscar-winning film composer, anti-nuclear activist, ambient minimalist: he treated genres the way Tokyo treats neighborhoods — as places to pass through, absorb, and leave changed.

In 1978 he co-founded Yellow Magic Orchestra with Haruomi Hosono and Yukihiro Takahashi, and effectively invented the template for electronic pop years before the rest of the world caught up. Then he walked away from pop stardom to score films, winning the Academy Award for The Last Emperor in 1988. Then he walked away from that, too.

What made him Tokyo's artist rather than just Japan's was the restlessness. The city reinvents itself every decade and discards anyone who won't keep up; Sakamoto reinvented himself faster than the city could, and the city kept him anyway.

Tokyo roots

Sakamoto's Tokyo is the city's experimental nervous system. YMO was built in Tokyo studios with gear most musicians hadn't seen yet; the Shibuya-kei generation of the '90s treated him as a patron saint; and after the 2011 Fukushima disaster, he became one of Japan's most visible anti-nuclear voices — organizing, protesting, and scoring the anxiety of a city that had to rethink its own power grid.

Walk through Shimokitazawa or Koenji today and his influence is ambient: in the record bars, in the modular-synth nights, in the film scores leaking from tiny cinemas. He died in March 2023, and the city mourned him the way it mourns infrastructure — because that's what he'd become.

One key track

"Merry Christmas, Mr. Lawrence" (1983). Written for the Nagisa Oshima film he also starred in, it's three minutes of piano that somehow contains an entire war. It remains the most covered piece of Japanese instrumental music ever written, and it still stops rooms.

Most artists have a sound. Sakamoto had a weather system, and Tokyo learned to live under it.

Start here

async (2017) — his late-career masterpiece of found sound and piano, made as he confronted his own mortality. Then 12 (2023), the quiet sketches he left behind. Play them back to back and you hear a man editing his own ending.
