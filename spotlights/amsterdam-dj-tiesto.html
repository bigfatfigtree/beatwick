ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · AMSTERDAM

Tiësto

The Breda kid who took trance to the Olympics and became the first DJ to outgrow the club entirely.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · FEBRUARY 20, 2026 · 2 MIN READ

Tijs Verwest was born in 1976 in Breda, in the Dutch south, and spent the nineties working his way up through the Netherlands' club circuit — residencies, mixtapes, the long apprenticeship. Then trance happened, and Tiësto happened to trance. By the early 2000s he was the biggest DJ on earth: number one in the DJ Mag Top 100 three years running, the first DJ to sell out a solo stadium show, and — the detail that still bends minds — the first DJ to perform at an Olympic opening ceremony, Athens 2004.

The Athens set is the hinge of his story. A DJ, alone on a podium, playing to billions of television viewers at the most-watched non-sporting event on the planet. It was the moment electronic music stopped asking for legitimacy and started assuming it. Tiësto didn't just ride the wave; he was the wave — the figure who proved a DJ could be a headliner, a brand, a stadium act.

Amsterdam is his spiritual home turf even if Breda is his birthplace: the Dutch dance establishment runs through the capital — the labels, the Amsterdam Dance Event, the whole infrastructure that turned the Netherlands into dance music's Saudi Arabia (all reserves, no apology). Tiësto is the founding father of that dynasty, the one Martin Garrix saw on television at age eight and decided to become.

Then came the second act, which most pioneers fumble. As trance faded, Tiësto didn't fade with it — he pivoted to big-room and EDM-pop, then to the sleek house of "The Business" (2020), a genuine global hit fifteen years after his supposed peak. Reinvention is the hardest trick in dance music, and he's pulled it off three times. The Breda kid is now a Las Vegas institution, and the Olympics gig doesn't even feel like the strangest line on the résumé anymore.

The key track

"Adagio for Strings" (2005). His remix of Samuel Barber's classical piece — yes, really — that turned a funeral march into the most euphoric record in trance history. It's absurd on paper and perfect on a dancefloor: the track that proved Tiësto could make anything soar.

Start here

Elements of Life (2007) for the imperial trance phase at full power. Then "The Business" (2020) for the second-act reinvention, and the In Search of Sunrise mix series for the long, slow-burn version of the legend.

The take: Tiësto didn't just become the world's biggest DJ — he invented the job description. Stadiums, Olympics, Vegas: every DJ headlining at that scale is working a playbook he wrote in Breda and perfected in Amsterdam.
