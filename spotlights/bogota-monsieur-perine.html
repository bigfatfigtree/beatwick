ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · BOGOTÁ

Monsieur Periné

Bogotá's swing-era dreamers who smuggled cumbia into a Parisian jazz club.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · FEBRUARY 19, 2026 · 2 MIN READ

Monsieur Periné formed in Bogotá in 2012 around singer Catalina García and guitarist Santiago Prieto, with a premise that sounded like a dare: what if 1930s French swing — Django Reinhardt's jazz manouche — crashed into Colombian cumbia, bolero, and bossa nova? They called the result "suin a la colombiana," and it turned out to be one of the most joyful sounds of the decade.

The records built the case: Hecho a Mano (2012), then Caja de Música (2015), produced by Eduardo Cabra of Calle 13, which won them the Latin Grammy for Best New Artist in 2015. Encanto Tropical (2018) and Bolero Apocalíptico (2023) — the latter a Latin Grammy winner for Best Alternative Music Album — kept expanding the palette without losing the swing.

Bogotá is in the bones of this band: the bohemian bar circuit, the Chapinero nights, the city's long romance with jazz and its deeper romance with the Caribbean. They've taken it everywhere since — Coachella, the Montreux Jazz Festival, stages on four continents — but the sound still feels like a Bogotá evening that decided to dress up.

The band's visual world is half the fun — vintage suits, floral crowns, a stage show that looks like a 1930s postcard that learned to dance cumbia. Bogotá's design crowd adopted them as mascots years ago. The rest of the world just adopted the songs, which was always the easier path.

The key track

"Nuestra Canción," written by García with Dominican singer Vicente García, released in 2015. A flower-strewn love song that became a slow-burn phenomenon — years after release it hit number one on the U.S. TikTok chart and racked up billions of views, introducing the band to a generation that wasn't born when it came out. Some songs are just patient.

Start here

"Nuestra Canción" first, then "Bailar Contigo" for the dancefloor, and their gorgeous bolero cover "Sabor a Mí" for the romantics. Then Caja de Música in full — it's the record where the whole idea clicks.

They dressed Colombian folklore in a tuxedo and taught it to dance like it was 1935. It worked.
