ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · MANILA

SB19

Trained like K-pop, sung in Filipino — SB19 built P-pop from a Manila practice room.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · APRIL 1, 2026 · 3 MIN READ

In 2018, a Korean entertainment company called ShowBT set up shop in Manila, held auditions, and put five Filipino trainees through the full K-pop regimen: years of dawn-to-midnight training, dorm life, monthly evaluations. The result was SB19 — Pablo, Stell, Josh, Ken, and Justin — and the result of the result was P-pop, a genre that barely existed before them.

"Go Up" went viral in 2019 on the strength of a dance-practice video. "Gento" (2023) turned them into a global concern. They became the first Filipino act nominated at the Billboard Music Awards and won Billboard's Fan Army Face-Off — twice. All of it sung largely in Filipino, all of it built in Manila.

Their fandom, A'TIN, operates with military precision — streaming parties, voting drives, charity projects under the group's name. It's the Manila way: turn devotion into infrastructure.

Manila roots

SB19's story is Manila's music industry growing up in public. The city had the talent and the hunger; what it lacked was the training infrastructure, so a Korean company imported it wholesale. The members have been open about the grind — the years of uncertainty, the debt, the almost-quitting — and that honesty is pure Manila: the city respects hustle more than polish.

Now the practice rooms they came up in have multiplied across the metro. P-pop groups are debuting monthly, and the A'TIN fandom is one of the most organized fan armies in Southeast Asia. SB19 didn't just become stars; they became an industry template, stamped "Made in Manila."

One key track

"Gento" (2023). The mining-town metaphor, the chant-along hook, the choreography that launched a thousand TikToks. It's the sound of a group that stopped asking permission — and the moment P-pop went from local curiosity to export.

The PAGTATAG! world tour then took the whole operation international — North America, Asia, sold-out rooms full of people who'd learned Tagalog choruses phonetically. The practice rooms in Manila are still where it starts; the world is just where it plays now.

They were trained like K-pop and marketed like a startup. They succeeded like neither — they succeeded like Manila.

The PAGTATAG! EP (2023) — six tracks including "Gento," and the tightest statement of what P-pop is: Filipino lyrics, Korean training, and confidence for days.
