ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · LONDON

Stormzy

The South London MC who took grime from YouTube freestyles to Glastonbury's Pyramid Stage — and made the whole country learn his name.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · OCTOBER 7, 2025 · 2 MIN READ

Before Stormzy, the plan for a grime MC was pirate radio, a mixtape, and a ceiling. Michael Omari — born in Croydon, raised in South London — skipped the whole ladder. In 2014 and 2015 he dropped the "Wicked Skengman" freestyle series straight onto YouTube: phone-camera visuals, no budget, bars like scaffolding. Millions watched. The ceiling turned out to be a skylight.

Grime's first wave came from pirate stations and estates in Bow and Tottenham, and Stormzy treats that lineage like scripture — Wiley is his godfather, Dizzee the proof of concept. But Stormzy's genius was reading the room of a new era. While the old guard fought for radio spins, he built a direct line to the audience. Gang Signs & Prayer (2017) debuted at number one in the UK — the first grime album to do it — and suddenly the sound of South London youth clubs was the sound of the charts, full stop.

Then came the coronation. In June 2019 he headlined Glastonbury's Pyramid Stage — the first Black British solo artist to do so — wearing a Union Jack stab vest designed by Banksy, backed by a ballet dancer interpreting "Vossi Bop." It was the most-watched Glastonbury set in the festival's history at the time. London has produced bigger pop stars. It has never produced a bigger moment.

What makes him a London artist in the fullest sense is what he did with the leverage. The #Merky Books imprint with Penguin, the Stormzy Scholarship funding Black British students at Cambridge, the Merky FC program opening football pathways for Black youth — he turned chart success into infrastructure. And he never softened the edges: his BRITs stage has been used to say exactly what he thinks of whoever's running the country.

The key track

"Shut Up" (2015). A freestyle over XTC's "Functions on the Low," filmed in a car park in South London, that became the highest-charting freestyle in UK history. Three minutes of pure dismissal — every bar a locked door. It didn't just break Stormzy; it proved the audience was already there, waiting for someone to speak their language at full volume.

Start here

Gang Signs & Prayer (2017). Half grime sermon, half gospel-tinged confession — the album that took the sound from the ends to the nation. "Big for Your Boots" for the energy, "Blinded by Your Grace" for the heart. It's the sound of a kid from Croydon realizing he was carrying a whole scene, and deciding to carry it properly.

The take: Stormzy didn't just win at grime — he changed what winning looked like. Every UK rapper with a direct line to their audience is walking a road he paved with a phone camera and a car park.
