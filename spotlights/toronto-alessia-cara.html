ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · TORONTO

Alessia Cara: Brampton's quiet storm

She sang about hating parties and won a Grammy. The GTA's most honest voice never had to shout.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · MAY 28, 2026 · 4 MIN READ

Alessia Caracciolo was posting acoustic covers from her bedroom in Brampton, Ontario when "Here" — a song about being miserable at a party — became one of 2015's defining hits. It was the anti-party anthem for everyone who'd ever stood in a corner holding a drink they didn't want, and it announced a songwriter with an almost uncomfortable level of honesty.

Know-It-All (2015) and The Pains of Growing (2018) built the case: a voice that cracks in the right places, lyrics about anxiety and insecurity that read like diary pages. In 2018 she won the Grammy for Best New Artist — the first Canadian to do it — beating the industry's chosen favorites by simply being the most believable person in the room.

Why Toronto claims her

Cara is the GTA's suburban success story — not the downtown cool of the Toronto sound, but the Brampton reality of strip malls and basement shows. She's proof the region's music economy extends past the 6ix's core, and her Italian-Canadian background adds another thread to the area's immigrant-kid songwriting tradition. She reps the suburbs the way Drake reps the city: completely, unapologetically, on every record.

Her openness about anxiety and mental health made her a different kind of role model — the pop star who tells you it's okay to leave the party early.

Toronto makes stars who perform confidence. Alessia Cara became a star by performing the exact opposite — and the suburbs showed up for it.

The catalog keeps deepening. The Pains of Growing earned a Juno for Album of the Year, and 2025's Love & Hyperbole found her writing with the confidence of someone who's survived the industry's hype cycle intact. Brampton's quiet storm never needed to get louder — she just needed the world to lean in closer.

Key track

"Here" — the breakout, all weary defiance over a sample of Isaac Hayes. "I guess right now you've got the last laugh" is the quietest mic drop in pop.

Start here

Know-It-All for the arrival, The Pains of Growing for the depth. Play "Scars to Your Beautiful" when you need reminding that the suburbs produce philosophers too.

Brampton gave pop its most honest voice. She gave it back permission to be uncomfortable.
