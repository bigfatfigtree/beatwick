ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · BOGOTÁ

Aterciopelados

Bogotá's alt-rock weirdos who made Andean weirdness anthemic.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · JUNE 2, 2026 · 2 MIN READ

Andrea Echeverri and Héctor Buitrago formed Aterciopelados in Bogotá in 1990, and Latin alternative music has been catching up ever since. Punk guitars, Andean flutes, drum machines, bolero rhythms detonated by distortion — and Echeverri's voice, by turns childlike and ferocious, holding the whole beautiful mess together.

The classics came fast: El Dorado (1995) and Caribe Atómico (1998) invented a template — Colombian roots music fed through a rock band with art-school brains and zero interest in anybody's rules. They were playing Rock al Parque, Bogotá's massive free rock festival, when the idea of Latin alternative barely had a name.

Bogotá is the band's natural habitat: the gray-skied, high-altitude capital where the Andes meet the underground. Echeverri, a ceramicist and visual artist as well as a singer, turned the band's whole aesthetic into a statement — feminist, ecological, gloriously strange. Three decades in, they're still the weirdos every new Bogotá band has to reckon with.

Echeverri's visual art and the band's later records — Oye (2006), Río (2008), Claroscura (2018) — kept the flame lit, and their feminist, ecological activism made them moral as well as musical leaders. They took Colombian alt-rock to festival stages across the Americas and Europe before "Latin alternative" was a marketing category. Young Bogotá bands still cover "Bolero Falaz" like a folk song. At this point, it basically is one. Three decades on, the weirdos won.

Bogotá's altitude seems to produce a specific kind of beautiful oddity, and Aterciopelados bottled it first. Every Latin alternative festival on the continent still books them as the act that proves the genre has roots — weird, wonderful, Andean roots.

The key track

"Bolero Falaz," from El Dorado. A bolero rhythm hijacked by electric guitars — the mission statement in four minutes. It shouldn't work: a swooning Latin ballad form played like a punk song. It works so well it became an anthem.

Start here

"Bolero Falaz" first, then "Florecita Rockera" for the pop instincts, then "Maligno" (2006) for the darker late period. Then El Dorado front to back — it's the Rosetta Stone of Colombian alt-rock.

They made the strangest music in the country and made the country sing along. That's not a career, that's a magic trick.
