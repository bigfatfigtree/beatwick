ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · LONDON

Adele

Tottenham's once-in-a-generation voice — the BRIT School graduate who outsold everyone and stayed herself.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · APRIL 16, 2026 · 2 MIN READ

Adele Laurie Blue Adkins was born in 1988 in Tottenham, North London, and grew up there — the same streets that produced grime's first wave. She took the other road out: the BRIT School in Croydon, the free performing-arts school that has produced an absurd share of British pop royalty. A friend posted her demo on MySpace. XL Recordings called. The rest is the best-selling story of the century.

19 (2008) introduced the voice — "Chasing Pavements," "Hometown Glory" — and won her two Grammys. Then 21 (2011) did something no album had done in decades: it sold over 30 million copies worldwide, spent 24 weeks at number one in the UK, and turned heartbreak into the biggest commercial force in music. "Rolling in the Deep" and "Someone Like You" weren't just hits; they were cultural weather.

The Tottenham connection matters because Adele never performed the pop-star vanishing act. She stayed funny, stayed blunt, stayed herself — the interviews are as beloved as the albums. 25 (2015) gave us "Hello" and the fastest-selling album in UK chart history. 30 (2021) went deeper and darker. The Las Vegas residency, "Weekends with Adele," sold out instantly and ran for two years. Sixteen Grammys, an Oscar, a BRITs icon award — the trophy cabinet needs its own postcode.

What's remarkable is the discipline of it: four albums in thirteen years, each one an event, each one recorded like the charts were an afterthought. In an era of content churn, Adele releases music the old way — when it's ready, when it matters — and the world rearranges its schedule. Tottenham's greatest export isn't a sound. It's a standard.

The key track

"Rolling in the Deep" (2010). The stomp, the gospel choir, the vocal that goes from wounded to vengeful in four minutes. It's the song that turned a breakup album into a global phenomenon — and it still hits like the first time.

Start here

21 (2011) — the landmark, the one that belongs in every conversation about the great albums. Then 25 for "Hello" and the imperial phase, and 30 when you want the grown-up version.

The take: Adele proved the old rules still work: write great songs, sing them like you mean it, disappear until the next one. Tottenham gave her the voice; the BRIT School gave her the stage. She did the rest herself.
