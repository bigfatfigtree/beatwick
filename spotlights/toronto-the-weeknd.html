ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · TORONTO

The Weeknd: Toronto after dark

From anonymous Scarborough mixtapes to the biggest song of the streaming era — Abel Tesfaye made the night sound expensive.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · FEBRUARY 19, 2026 · 4 MIN READ

In 2011, three free mixtapes appeared on the internet under a misspelled name, with no photos, no interviews, no identity at all. The Weeknd was a ghost from Toronto's east end — Scarborough, to be exact — singing drugged-out R&B over samples that sounded like the city at 4 a.m. By the time anyone saw his face, House of Balloons had already rewired the genre.

Abel Tesfaye's arc is the streaming era's defining ascent: from anonymity to Beauty Behind the Madness (2015), from "Can't Feel My Face" ubiquity to the Super Bowl LV halftime show in 2021, from Toronto kid to global headliner. Then came "Blinding Lights" — the synth-driven juggernaut that became the best-performing song in Billboard Hot 100 history, spending 90 weeks on the chart. Ninety.

Why Toronto claims him

The Weeknd is the dark twin of Drake's Toronto — same OVO-adjacent orbit, opposite energy. Where Drake made the 6ix sound like a victory lap, Tesfaye made it sound like the afterparty you shouldn't have stayed for. His early mystique was pure Toronto indie lore: the blog-era rollout, the Mod Club shows, the city's tight-knit producer scene building a sound the world would spend years chasing.

He's since retired the Weeknd moniker in favor of his birth name, Abel Tesfaye — a very Toronto move, honestly: leave the stage name in the rearview and keep moving.

Drake gave Toronto its swagger. The Weeknd gave it its shadows — and the shadows turned out to be worth billions of streams.

The Weeknd era officially closed with Hurry Up Tomorrow (2025) — an album and an accompanying film that wrapped the persona in grand, cinematic fashion. Abel Tesfaye walked away from the name that made him famous at the absolute peak, which might be the most Weeknd move of all: disappear at the height of the party, and let the mythology do the rest.

Key track

"Blinding Lights" — the '80s-synth fever dream that refused to leave the charts for nearly two years. Say what you want about ubiquity; the hook is indestructible.

Start here

House of Balloons for the origin myth, After Hours for the neon-noir peak. Play it driving the DVP at night with the skyline lit up — that's the intended venue.

Toronto's night has a soundtrack, and it sounds like Abel Tesfaye singing to an empty highway.
