ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · MONTREAL

Arcade Fire: Montreal's indie-rock miracle

A funeral that became a coronation: how a Montreal collective took Funeral from Mile End lofts to a Grammy for Album of the Year.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · NOVEMBER 27, 2025 · 4 MIN READ

In 2004, a sprawling collective from Montreal's Mile End released a debut album called Funeral and accidentally reset indie rock's ambitions. Arcade Fire — Win Butler and Régine Chassagne's ever-expanding ensemble of multi-instrumentalists — made grief sound like a parade, and the parade never really stopped.

The Montreal part isn't decoration. The band formed in the city's cheap-rent early-2000s Mile End, rehearsing in lofts alongside a whole ecosystem of bands, and recorded with the same communal spirit that made the neighborhood a petri dish. Cheap apartments plus long winters plus bilingual weirdness: that formula built the scene, and Arcade Fire became its export.

Why Montreal claims them

When The Suburbs won the Grammy for Album of the Year in 2011 — beating Eminem, Lady Gaga, and Katy Perry — it was the ultimate Mile End fairy tale: a record about suburban alienation, recorded partly in a church, made by a band that still felt like a neighborhood project. The city treats them like returning royalty; their shows at local venues are civic events, and the francophone-anglophone mix of the band mirrors Montreal's own beautiful friction.

Local lore: the band's early shows at tiny rooms like Casa del Popolo are the stuff of legend, the kind of gigs people lie about having attended.

Arcade Fire proved a band could stay weird, stay collective, stay Montreal — and still beat the entire pop industry at its own awards show.

The band's conscience runs deeper than the music. Régine Chassagne's Haitian heritage shaped albums like Reflektor, and her KANPE foundation has raised millions for Haiti — the other half of Montreal's diaspora story. Arcade Fire never treated success as an exit ramp from the city's values; they treated it as a bigger stage for them.

Key track

"Wake Up" — the Funeral closer that starts as a whisper and ends as a stadium chant. It's been the band's calling card for two decades: communal, cathartic, impossible to sing alone.

Start here

Funeral (2004), all the way through, on a cold night. Then Neon Bible for the darker cathedral phase. If you can, catch them live — the show is the argument for the band's existence.

Montreal gave Arcade Fire cheap lofts and long winters. They gave it back as the most ambitious rock band of their generation.
