ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · KINGSTON

Bob Marley

The prophet of Trench Town who made a tiny island's music the planet's second language.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · OCTOBER 21, 2025 · 2 MIN READ

Robert Nesta Marley was born in 1945 in Nine Mile, St. Ann — but Bob Marley the artist was manufactured in Kingston, in the government yards of Trench Town, where he learned guitar between rehearsals and learned hunger from everything else. Every reggae singer since has lived in the house he built; most of them know it.

The Wailers formed in Kingston in 1963 — Marley, Peter Tosh, and Bunny Wailer — and cut their first sides for Coxsone Dodd's Studio One, where the single "Simmer Down" went to number one in Jamaica. Kingston gave Marley his apprenticeship: the sound-system dances, the rude-boy era, the session-musician grind. By the early seventies, with Island Records behind them, the Wailers turned roots reggae into an international language.

The city landmarks are still there, still working. Number 56 Hope Road is Tuff Gong — his home, his studio, now a museum and a functioning recording room where the walls hum. Two nights after gunmen tried to kill him at that house in December 1976, Marley played the Smile Jamaica concert anyway. Two years later, at the One Love Peace Concert, he joined the hands of Jamaica's rival political leaders on stage. No other musician has ever made a gesture like that land.

The London exile years get mythologized, but the Kingston years did the heavy lifting: the Wailers' harmonies were worked out in Trench Town yards, not studios, and the political courage — playing Smile Jamaica two days after being shot — came from a city where music and survival were always the same skill.

The key track

"One Love / People Get Ready," from Exodus (1977), recorded in London during his exile after the shooting. Marley took Curtis Mayfield's civil-rights hymn and rebuilt it as a global singalong — and then the album around it became, in Time magazine's judgment, the best record of the twentieth century. The rhythm guitar's offbeat chop on that track is the most imitated strum in pop history.

Start here

Play "One Love / People Get Ready" loud enough that the neighbors get a vote. Then run Exodus front to back, then Kaya for the sweetness. Want the militant roots? Catch a Fire and Burnin', in that order. That's a week of listening that will recalibrate your ears.

He died in 1981 at thirty-six. Kingston never really accepted it — he's on every wall, every corner bar, every sound system. The city didn't lose him; it just turned him into infrastructure.
