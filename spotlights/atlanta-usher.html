ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · ATLANTA

Usher: Atlanta's king of R&B

He moved to Atlanta as a teenager and never left the throne. Thirty years later, the Confessions still haven't been topped.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · APRIL 2, 2026 · 4 MIN READ

Usher Raymond was a thirteen-year-old from Texas with a voice too big for his age when his family moved him to Atlanta — the city with LaFace Records, the label that would turn him into a star. It took a few years and a growth spurt, but by 1997's My Way, the teenager was a headliner. By Confessions (2004), he was the biggest R&B artist on the planet.

Confessions sold over a million copies in its first week — a number R&B albums simply don't do — and "Yeah!" (with Lil Jon and Ludacris) fused Atlanta's crunk energy with pop R&B so completely that the rest of the decade had no choice but to follow. He mentored a kid named Justin Bieber, headlined the Super Bowl LVIII halftime show in 2024, and did a hundred-show Las Vegas residency that reminded everyone the throne was never actually vacant.

Why Atlanta claims him

Usher is the connective tissue of Atlanta music: the LaFace era's crown jewel, the bridge between '90s R&B and the trap-soul wave, the guy every Atlanta singer from Ciara to Summer Walker cites as the blueprint. He stayed in the city, invested in it, and became part of its civic furniture — the rare megastar who never needed to pretend Atlanta was a stepping stone.

Local lore: his roller-skating halftime performance broke the internet's brain for a week, which is just what happens when Atlanta's king decides to show off.

Atlanta runs on two things: bass and Usher's falsetto. The bass changes. The falsetto is forever.

The third act keeps getting better. The 2024 Super Bowl halftime show — roller skates and all — was a coronation in front of a hundred million people, and the Coming Home album and its massive tour proved the voice hasn't lost a step. Thirty years in, Usher isn't a legacy act. He's the standard every R&B singer still gets measured against.

Key track

"Yeah!" — Lil Jon's crunk siren, Ludacris's verse, and Usher gliding over the top like it's effortless. The song that made Atlanta's underground the world's mainstream.

Start here

Confessions for the imperial phase, 8701 for the come-up. Then watch the Super Bowl LVIII halftime show to see what three decades of stagecraft looks like at full power.

Kings come and go in R&B. Atlanta's has held the title since the '90s — and he's not giving it up.
