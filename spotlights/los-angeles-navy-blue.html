ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · LOS ANGELES

NAVY BLUE

Los Angeles-born skater turned underground-rap confessor — drumless loops, warm jazz samples, and the quietest fire in the city.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · JANUARY 7, 2026 · 3 MIN READ

Sage Elsesser became famous twice before he turned 25 — first as a pro skateboarder for Supreme, canonized in the 2013 video Cherry, and then, quietly, as Navy Blue, one of the most quietly devastating rappers alive. Since 2015 he's been making insular, drumless, jazz-sampled rap on his own terms: eight-plus albums of existential confessionals, released largely independently on his own Freedom Sounds label. He's the guy at the end of Frank Ocean's Blonde talking about the theremin. He produced for Earl Sweatshirt. He raps like he's reading from a journal he's not sure you'll understand.

The debut studio album, Àdá Irin (2020), set the template: unhurried loops, internal ruminations, and a single guest verse from Brooklyn cult legend Ka — a mentor relationship that shaped Navy Blue's whole ethos. The industry noticed anyway. In 2023 he signed to Def Jam, released Ways of Knowing, and was dropped a year later — which, in retrospect, reads like the best thing that ever happened to the discography. Memoirs in Armour (2024) and The Sword & the Soaring (2026) arrived on Freedom Sounds, fully independent, reuniting him with longtime collaborator Earl Sweatshirt on "24 Gospel." The major-label detour lasted a blink. The independence is permanent.

WHY LOS ANGELES CLAIMS HIM

Elsesser grew up in Mid City, hung around Fairfax's skate shops before they were a global brand story, and came up alongside Odd Future's orbit — Earl Sweatshirt has been his best friend since elementary school. That lineage runs through everything: LA's skater-rap continuum, the Fairfax counterculture, the city's tradition of doing the loudest thing in the quietest way possible. He may travel, but the music's center of gravity is Los Angeles.

THE KEY TRACK

"24 Gospel" (2026) — the Earl Sweatshirt reunion, and the clearest proof that two of underground rap's most interior voices make each other louder. Warm, meditative, spiritually heavy. For the older stuff, go to "In Good Hands" with Ka: two generations of quiet rap, one lineage.

THE SOUND

Drumless loops. Diffuse, immersive jazz samples. A voice that rarely rises above a murmur. Navy Blue's records reward attention like nobody else's — play them loud and they stay small; play them quiet and they fill the room. It's the most patient music in rap, and in a city built on noise, that's a radical act.

START HERE

Àdá Irin, then Memoirs in Armour, then The Sword & the Soaring. When you're converted, the Saga of Sage box set is the deep end.

Navy Blue chose the independent path twice — first by default, then by conviction. Los Angeles' quietest voice is also one of its truest.
