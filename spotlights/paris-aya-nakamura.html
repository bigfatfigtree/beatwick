ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · PARIS

Aya Nakamura

The banlieue singer who made French the language of global R&B — one "Djadja" at a time.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · FEBRUARY 10, 2026 · 2 MIN READ

Aya Danioko was born in 1995 in Bamako, Mali, and raised in Aulnay-sous-Bois, in the Seine-Saint-Denis department north of Paris — the 93, the banlieue the French media loves to fear and the French charts love to mine. She took her stage name from Hiro Nakamura, the Heroes character. Then she became the biggest French-language pop star on the planet, which is a better superpower.

The breakthrough was "Djadja" (2018): a slinking, deadpan kiss-off over an airy Afro-pop beat that became the most-streamed French-language song in the world and a genuine global hit — covered, remixed, and sung phonetically by people who didn't speak a word of French. The album Nakamura followed, then Aya (2020) and DNK (2023), each one tightening the formula: minimal beats, conversational delivery, hooks that lodge in your skull.

Her Paris story is the banlieue story. The 93 has been France's hit factory for two decades — NTM, PNL, and now Nakamura — producing the sounds the center pretends it discovered. She sings in a French peppered with Bambara, Nouchi, and verlan, the actual language of the Paris suburbs, and refused to sand it down for anyone. When the French establishment came for her ahead of the Paris 2024 Olympics — a racist backlash against her rumored opening-ceremony slot — she performed anyway, alongside the Republican Guard, singing Aznavour. The ratings were enormous. The point was made.

What she changed is structural: she proved French-language R&B could travel without translation, that the banlieue's sound was the French sound. Every Francophone pop star working now is downstream of "Djadja."

The key track

"Djadja" (2018). The shrug heard round the world — a man gets dismissed in under three minutes over one of the decade's most economical beats. It's the sound of absolute self-possession, and it made French cool in countries that had never cared.

Start here

Nakamura (2018) — the breakthrough album, "Djadja" and "Copines" back to back. Then DNK (2023) for the leaner, meaner current version, and her Olympic ceremony performance if you want to see what vindication looks like.

The take: Aya Nakamura took the language of the Paris suburbs global without changing a syllable. The banlieue doesn't need Paris's permission — it never did. She just made the rest of the world listen.
