ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · SÃO PAULO

Criolo

São Paulo's conscience with a microphone — rap that sounds like rain on concrete.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · NOVEMBER 4, 2025 · 2 MIN READ

Kleber Cavalcante Gomes — Criolo — was born in São Paulo in 1975 and came up through the city's underground hip hop scene in the nineties and two-thousands, a fixture of the legendary rap crew RZO and the battle circuit that raised a generation of paulista MCs. He spent a decade as the scene's best-kept secret before deciding, in his mid-thirties, to become its conscience.

The breakthrough was Nó na Orelha (2011), produced by Daniel Ganjaman — a record that refused to stay in one lane, folding samba, reggae, and afrobeat into rap without asking permission. It turned Criolo from a rapper's rapper into a national figure, and it permanently raised the bar for what Brazilian hip hop was allowed to sound like. Convoque Seu Buda (2014) and the samba-drunk Espiral de Ilusão (2017) kept pushing outward.

Nobody writes São Paulo the way Criolo does. His songs are full of the city's weather and weight — the gray skies, the traffic, the cold reserve of the paulista, the tenderness underneath it. He headlines Virada Cultural, the city's twenty-four-hour culture festival, and the SESC stages; he's as much a civic institution now as he is a rapper.

His live shows are famous for their range — he'll move from a whispered samba to a full-throated rap inside a single set, backed by a band that can follow him anywhere. São Paulo's critics sometimes call him the city's conscience; the compliment undersells the musicianship. The man could rap the phone book and make it swing.

The key track

"Não Existe Amor em SP," from Nó na Orelha. A love song written as a complaint: rain hammering the avenue, the city refusing to hold you. It's the most quoted São Paulo anthem in music — the rare track that names a city's emotional weather and gets it exactly right. Play it on a gray day and the whole metropolis makes sense.

Start here

Run "Não Existe Amor em SP" on repeat until the rain feels personal. Then play Nó na Orelha front to back — it's one of the great Brazilian albums of the century. Bonus round: "Bogotá," from Convoque Seu Buda, a track named for another city on this very site. Criolo gets around.

Most rappers describe their city. Criolo indicts his, forgives it, and describes it again — all in the same verse.
