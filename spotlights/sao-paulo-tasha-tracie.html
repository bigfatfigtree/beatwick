ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · SÃO PAULO

TASHA & TRACIE

São Paulo's twin rap queens stitch funk carioca to trap and rap, rhyming about Black culture, the periphery, and pure self-esteem — with Ludmilla, Djonga, and Gloria Groove features to prove it.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · NOVEMBER 12, 2025 · 3 MIN READ

Tasha and Tracie Okereke made São Paulo's rap scene make room for them. The twin sisters' first EP, Rouff (2019), and the follow-up Directoria (2021), fuse rap and trap with funk carioca — and their verses on Black culture, periphery life, sex, fashion, and confidence hit so hard that Ludmilla, Djonga, and Gloria Groove all came calling for features.

It's rare for a duo to arrive this fully formed. Tasha and Tracie write about being Black women in Brazil with the swagger of people who have never once asked permission, switching between Portuguese slang, grime-inflected flows, and R&B hooks like they were born doing it. Their presence offstage is the same thesis — fashion, art, and activism folded into one unmissable package.

WHY SÃO PAULO CLAIMS THEM

They're from the city, and their music is the city's friction made sound: ostentação-era funk meeting conscious rap meeting the queer-inclusive, club-going SP that doesn't fit into one zip code. They've played São Paulo stages from SESC Bom Retiro to packed parties, and every room they touch gets louder.

THE KEY TRACK

"Amarrou." It's the song their listeners keep returning to — a slow-burn flex where the twins' chemistry is the instrument. Two voices, one thought process, zero wasted bars. If you only hear one track, make it this one.

THE BUZZ

Those co-signs aren't courtesy features. The twins landed on Ludmilla's Vilã, Djonga's Lady Leste, and Gloria Groove's O Dono do Lugar because Brazil's biggest rappers heard something that cuts through. Rolling Stone Brazil sat them down for an interview; the scene keeps booking them because they move crowds, not because they're a novelty act.

And it goes beyond the booth. Tasha and Tracie work in fashion, art, and activism as much as music — their visuals are as considered as their bars, and their message of confidence and self-esteem for Black Brazilian women travels further than any single. Rolling Stone Brazil's interview with the twins dug into exactly that: they're building an aesthetic world, not just dropping singles, and the fashion press noticed as early as the music press did.

START HERE

"Amarrou," then "Sou Má" with Ludmilla, then the whole Directoria EP front to back. Then go back to Rouff and hear where the confidence started.

Tasha & Tracie didn't break into São Paulo rap. They redesigned the door.
