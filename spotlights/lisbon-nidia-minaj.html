ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · LISBON

NÍDIA MINAJ

The kuduro queen of Príncipe Discos who learned beat-making from YouTube tutorials and became Lisbon's most explosive electronic export.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · AUGUST 12, 2026 · 3 MIN READ

Listen → Listen on Spotify

No official “Limite” video could be verified — start with the record.

Nídia started in high school, in an all-girl dance group called Kaninas Squad, dancing kuduro — the ultra-rhythmic Angolan dance music — in Vale da Amoreira. Then she did what the whole Príncipe generation did: went deep into YouTube tutorials, learned to make beats herself, and started firing off tracks so explosive that Lisbon's most important electronic label came calling. Dazed interviewed her when she was 17 and still in school. Three years later she'd toured the world, landed on Warp Records' Cargaa compilation, and released her debut album Nídia é Má, Nídia é Fudida (2017) on Príncipe.

Her sound is kuduro run through a blender: rapid-fire percussion, chopped voices, carnival melodies shredded into club weapons, with track titles that don't ask for permission. The Danger EP (2015) announced her; the album made her the label's breakout; and her DJ sets — from Unsound to the world's festival stages — remain some of the most physical hours in electronic music. She's now based in Bordeaux, but the music still pines for the ghetto she came from: “I Miss My Guetto,” indeed.

WHY LISBON CLAIMS HER

Príncipe Discos is Lisbon's electronic conscience — the label SPIN called the source of “the best party music in the world” — and Nídia is its most beloved story: a kid from the suburbs who taught herself production and became the global face of batida and kuduro's mutation. Every Príncipe night at Musicbox still carries her DNA.

THE KEY TRACK

“Limite” (2015). From Danger: hyperactive drums, shredded carnival synths, pure adrenaline. It's the track that made the international press realize Lisbon's suburbs were producing the most futuristic dance music on earth.

THE VOICE

Percussion as voice. Nídia's tracks are built from drums first — polyrhythmic, relentless, joyful — with vocal chops and synth stabs as decoration. “Hard work pays off,” she told Dazed, and you can hear the work ethic in every bar: nothing is loose, everything hits. It's the sound of someone who learned by doing, publicly, at 140 BPM.

START HERE

Danger for the explosion, Nídia é Má, Nídia é Fudida for the full statement, and the Dazed mix for the DJ. Then find a Príncipe night and experience the context at proper volume.
