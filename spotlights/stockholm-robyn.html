ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · STOCKHOLM

Robyn

Pop's patron saint of crying in the club — Stockholm's Robyn wrote the blueprint and never needed the crown.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · SEPTEMBER 8, 2026 · 2 MIN READ

Robin Miriam Carlsson was born in Stockholm in 1979 and signed her first record deal at fifteen — a teen-pop career ("Show Me Love," "Do You Really Want Me") that could have been the whole story. Instead she walked away from the major-label machine, founded her own Konichiwa Records, and spent the next two decades making the most emotionally intelligent pop music on earth. The industry's loss was everybody's gain.

Robyn (2005) was the reset — "With Every Heartbeat," produced with Kleerup, announced the new era: electronic, vulnerable, and utterly her own. Then came Body Talk (2010), released as three mini-albums in one year, and with it "Dancing on My Own" — the sad-banger to end all sad-bangers, a song about watching your ex with someone else that somehow became the decade's defining dancefloor anthem. It's been covered, sampled, and cried to in roughly equal measure ever since.

Stockholm is the water she swims in: the same pop-production ecosystem that produced Max Martin and Cheiron, but Robyn took the craft and pointed it inward. Where the hit factory writes for the world, she writes for the 2 a.m. version of herself — and the world comes to her. Her influence is everywhere in modern pop's emotional turn: the sad girls, the crying-in-the-club canon, all of it downstream of Body Talk.

She's also pop's great refusenik: no desperate trend-chasing, no algorithm-baiting, just records when she has something to say (Honey, 2018, was worth the eight-year wait). In an industry that rewards volume, Robyn rewards patience — and the patience is always repaid.

The key track

"Dancing on My Own" (2010). The piano chords, the kick drum, the vocal that cracks exactly where it should. It's the rare song that's both the saddest and the most euphoric thing in the room — pop's perfect paradox, and her masterpiece.

Start here

Body Talk (2010) — the full arc, heartbreak to healing, in under an hour. Then Honey (2018) for the sensual, late-night comedown, and "With Every Heartbeat" for the moment it all began.

The take: Robyn made vulnerability the hardest thing in pop — and proved Stockholm's hit machine could produce an auteur, not just songwriters. The crown was never the point. The songs were.
