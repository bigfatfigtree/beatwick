ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · SÃO PAULO

Emicida

The battle-rap killer who built an empire and named an album after hope.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · JANUARY 21, 2026 · 2 MIN READ

Leandro Roque de Oliveira was born in São Paulo in 1985, and his stage name started as a portmanteau of "emcee" and "homicide" — earned, the story goes, by killing opponents in freestyle battles across the city. He later turned it into an acronym: Enquanto Minha Imaginação Compuser Insanidades, Domino a Arte — as long as my imagination composes insanities, I dominate the art. Both versions are true.

He came up through São Paulo's ferocious battle circuit, winning so often it stopped being a contest and started being a coronation. In 2009 he and his brother Evandro Fióti founded Laboratório Fantasma, the independent label and creative house that became an institution of paulista hip hop — proof that the periphery could build its own industry instead of begging the center for a seat.

The records kept leveling up: O Glorioso Retorno de Quem Nunca Esteve Aqui (2013), and then AmarElo (2019) — a title fusing amar (to love) and amarelo (yellow), a meditation on Black joy and resistance that won the Latin Grammy for Best Portuguese Language Rock or Alternative Album in 2020. Emicida made hope sound like the hardest thing in rap. Because it is.

The AmarElo era also produced a Netflix documentary, AmarElo: É Tudo pra Ontem (2020), staged on the steps of São Paulo's Theatro Municipal — the album's thesis turned into cinema, with the city's Black history as the backdrop. Emicida doesn't just release records. He stages arguments, and he wins them.

The key track

"AmarElo," featuring Majur and Pabllo Vittar, built on a sample of Belchior's "Sujeito de Sorte." It's the thesis of the album in four minutes: tenderness as strategy, love as a political act, and a chorus big enough to hold a movement. The rare message song that never lectures — it just glows.

Start here

Play "AmarElo" with the lights low. Then the whole album, then back to "O Glorioso Retorno de Quem Nunca Esteve Aqui" to hear the hunger years. "Levanta e Anda" (with Rael) is the morning-after anthem.

He named himself after killing MCs and ended up writing lullabies for a movement. That's called range.
