ARTIST SPOTLIGHT · JOHANNESBURG

AYMOS

A church choir kid from Tembisa who invented Afro-Yano — amapiano with soul and warmth — and sang his way onto every anthem the genre produced.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · APRIL 29, 2026 · 3 MIN READ

Listen → Listen on Spotify

“Emcimbini” has no official video — start with the record.

Babili Amos Shili started in the Tembisa church choir and worked his way up through backing vocals and session piano before amapiano found him. When it did, it changed both of them. His 2020 collaborative EP with Mas Musiq, Shonamalanga, picked up two South African Music Awards nominations. Then "Emcimbini" detonated — produced by Kabza De Small and DJ Maphorisa, featuring Samthing Soweto, Mas Musiq and Myztrol — and suddenly Aymos was the voice of the genre's biggest crossover moment.

His debut album, Yimi Lo (September 2021), was a 14-track statement that gave his sound a name: Afro-Yano, amapiano with soul and warmth. It pulled in the Major League DJz, Josiah De Disciple, DBN Gogo, Boohle, Focalistic and Zakes Bantwini — and landed him on the cover of Apple Music's Isgubhu. "Fatela" with Ami Faku (2022) went multi-platinum. His second album, Impilo (August 2024), led by "10K Yey'nkomo" with Mas Musiq, Samthing Soweto and Sha Sha, confirmed the trajectory: in a genre built on producers, Aymos is the singer the producers call first.

WHY JOBURG CLAIMS HIM

Tembisa is on the East Rand — close enough to feel Joburg's pulse, far enough to sing about it from the outside. That distance is his superpower: Aymos writes amapiano like a soul record, all confession and warmth, while the log drums do the dancing. He's the genre's conscience with a choir-boy past.

THE KEY TRACK

"Emcimbini" (2021). The Kabza De Small and DJ Maphorisa production is seismic, but it's Aymos who gives the anthem its ache — a voice that sounds like it's carrying a whole township's prayers into the club. The song that made him unavoidable.

AFRO-YANO

His coinage, his lane. Where much of amapiano leans on groove and repetition, Aymos insists on songs — verses that build, melodies that resolve, lyrics about love and struggle that stick around after the bassline stops. "Fatela" with Ami Faku is the proof of concept: two voices, one ache, multi-platinum.

START HERE

"Emcimbini," then Yimi Lo for the full Afro-Yano manifesto, then "Fatela" and Impilo for where the sound went next.

Amapiano had the drums. Aymos gave it a voice.
