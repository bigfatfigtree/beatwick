SCENE REPORT · STOCKHOLM

Stockholm Writes Everyone's Pop Songs

How a tiny Stockholm studio built the modern hit — and why half your favorite songs were written within ten miles of the ABBA museum.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · JULY 16, 2026 · 5 MIN READ

Pick a pop song — any pop song — from the last thirty years, and there's a decent chance a Swede wrote it. Not produced it, not remixed it: wrote it, in a room in Stockholm, probably on a Tuesday. Britney's "...Baby One More Time." Kelly Clarkson's "Since U Been Gone." The Weeknd's "Blinding Lights." Taylor Swift's "Blank Space." The most successful songwriter of the modern era isn't from Los Angeles or London. He's Martin Sandberg, from Stenhamra, Sweden, and you know him as Max Martin.

The origin story starts in 1992, when a Stockholm DJ named Denniz Pop — born Dag Volle — founded Cheiron Studios. Denniz had a theory: pop was a craft, not a mystery, and it could be engineered like anything else. He took on a young protégé, Martin Sandberg, taught him the formula, and together they built the machine. "...Baby One More Time" (1998) was the proof of concept — a song so perfectly constructed it still sounds inevitable. Denniz died of cancer that same year, at 35. Cheiron closed in 2000. The students had already graduated.

Max Martin's numbers are the kind that break databases: more Billboard Hot 100 number ones as a songwriter than anyone in history except John Lennon and Paul McCartney. Read that again. A kid from the Stockholm suburbs out-wrote almost everyone who ever lived, by treating the three-minute pop song as an industrial product and himself as its most obsessive engineer.

The assembly line

Cheiron's real product wasn't songs — it was songwriters. Shellback (Karl Johan Schuster) came up through the Stockholm rock scene and became Martin's right hand. Savan Kotecha, Bloodshy & Avant, RedOne — the credits of the 2000s and 2010s read like a Stockholm phone book. The method was consistent: melody first, track second, ego nowhere. Swedish songwriters don't do auteur tortured-genius. They do shifts.

And the pipeline starts earlier than the studios. Sweden's municipal music schools — the kommunala musikskolan system — put instruments in kids' hands for pocket money. English fluency is near-universal, so Swedish writers think in the language of global pop natively. Melodifestivalen, the country's Eurovision selection circus, functions as a national songwriting combine, auditioning hooks in front of millions every spring. ABBA proved a Swedish act could conquer the world; Cheiron proved Sweden could conquer it without leaving the studio.

The Stockholm sound

Here's the twist: for a city that writes everyone's songs, Stockholm barely has a sound of its own. That's the point. The Swedish method is camouflage — write the perfect Weeknd song, the perfect Taylor song, the perfect Ariana song, and disappear into the credits. The city's own artists (Robyn, Tove Lo, Avicii) are the exceptions that prove the rule: even Stockholm's stars sound like they could be from anywhere, because the craft is the identity.

Los Angeles has the stars. London has the attitude. Stockholm has the factory — and the factory has never closed.

There's a Swedish concept, Jantelagen, that roughly translates to: don't think you're special. It's usually cited as a national neurosis. In the Stockholm studios, it's a business model. No ego in the room means the song wins every argument. The hook doesn't care who wrote it.

The machine keeps running

Three decades after Cheiron, the system is self-perpetuating. Max Martin's MXM Studios still operates out of Stockholm. The next generation — Oscar Holter, Ilya Salmanzadeh — learned at the same desks. The city's songwriting camps are the industry's open secret: fly in an American star, pair them with two Swedes and a piano, fly home with a hit.

Every city has a sound. Stockholm's sound is whatever's number one this week — because Stockholm probably wrote it. The ABBA museum gets the tourists. The studios get the royalties.

The take: Stockholm industrialized the pop song without ever making it sound industrial. Denniz Pop's little studio closed in 2000; its graduates have run global pop ever since. The hit factory with no smokestacks — just melodies, melodies, melodies.
