VENUE CULTURE · MELBOURNE

Melbourne’s Band Rooms Are the Real Music Industry

Forget the labels and the algorithms. Melbourne's music gets made in sticky-carpet band rooms — and the circuit is healthier than ever.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · SEPTEMBER 30, 2026 · 6 MIN READ

The most important A&R office in Australian music has sticky carpet, a front bar that smells like 1987, and a strict policy of not caring who you are. It's called the Tote, it sits on Johnston Street in Collingwood, and on any given Tuesday it will show you the future of Melbourne music for the price of a pot.

This city's industry isn't in boardrooms. It's in band rooms — the pub back-rooms and corner hotels where three bands play for forty people and the headliner is sometimes worse than the opener. Courtney Barnett, King Gizzard, Amyl and the Sniffers: all of them did their apprenticeships here, in rooms where the PA is held together by hope and the crowd will tell you exactly what they think.

The rooms have their own lore: the Tote's "don't be a dickhead" ethos, the Corner's front-bar regulars who've seen everything twice, the Forum's art-deco grandeur making every band feel bigger than they are. Ask a Melbourne musician where they learned their trade and they'll name a room, not a school.

The circuit is the label

Melbourne's band-room circuit functions like a record label that nobody owns. The Tote (saved from closure by community campaigns — twice), the Corner Hotel in Richmond, the Forum's grand old theatre, the Northcote Social Club: each room has its own bookers, its own regulars, its own idea of what's good. A band that can win all four rooms can win anywhere.

The economics are punk and proud of it. Door deals, cheap Tuesday slots for baby bands, all-ages matinees. Nobody's getting rich; everybody's getting tight. Melbourne bands tour like they rehearse — constantly — because the circuit demands it. By the time the industry notices a Melbourne act, the act has already played two hundred shows and doesn't need the industry's notes.

Sticky carpet as strategy

Other cities gentrified their live music into arenas and festivals. Melbourne kept the middle — the 200-cap rooms where bands learn to be bands. When lockouts and noise complaints killed Sydney's scene and Brisbane's venues fell to developers, Melbourne's councils mostly held the line, and the city's musicians repaid them by never leaving.

The rooms have their own lore: the Tote's fiercely protected dive-bar ethos, the Corner's front-bar regulars who've seen everything twice, the Forum's art-deco grandeur making every band feel bigger than they are. Ask a Melbourne musician where they learned the trade and they'll name a room, not a school.

And the pipeline keeps feeding itself. The same Tuesday-night slots that raised Barnett and Gizzard are raising the next wave right now — kids with cheap guitars and expensive opinions, learning in public the way every Melbourne great did.

Sydney has the harbour. Melbourne has the Tote. Only one of them ever broke a band.

The result is a scene that exports finished products. When Amyl and the Sniffers detonate a Glastonbury stage, they're running a set refined across a hundred Collingwood nights. When King Gizzard plays three hours without repeating a song, that's band-room stamina scaled up. The world sees the festival headline; Melbourne saw the Tuesday.

The rooms are the point

Here's the quiet truth the streaming era keeps missing: scenes are physical. Algorithms can surface a song, but they can't teach a nineteen-year-old drummer to hold a room, or a songwriter to read forty drunk people in real time. The band rooms do that. They're schools with beer taps.

So if you want to know what Melbourne sounds like next year, don't check the charts. Check the gig guide for a Tuesday in Collingwood. The future is playing third on the bill, and the PA is held together by hope.
