SCENE REPORT · LAGOS

Afrobeats Ate the World. Lagos Barely Noticed

The world thinks it just discovered Afrobeats. Lagos has been running this playbook for twenty years — and the city is already bored with the hype cycle.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · OCTOBER 14, 2026 · 4 MIN READ

Somewhere in the last few years, the global music industry held a meeting and decided Afrobeats was "the next big thing." Lagos checked the calendar. It has been the current big thing since before most of those executives could find Nigeria on a map.

Let's get the genealogy straight, because the industry won't. Fela Kuti invented Afrobeat — no "s" — in the 1970s: long, furious, political jams from the Afrika Shrine that treated the groove as a weapon. What the world calls Afrobeats — with the "s" — is the 21st-century Lagos invention: highlife DNA spliced with hip-hop, dancehall, R&B, and whatever else floated through the city's clubs. Different genre, same city. The confusion is the industry's; the receipts are Lagos's.

The playbook was written in the 2000s and 2010s by a generation the West ignored in real time. 2Baba (then 2Face Idibia) made "African Queen" a continental anthem. D'banj, P-Square, and Wizkid's "Ojuelegba" — a song about surviving Lagos traffic that became a diaspora lullaby — built the infrastructure. By the time Burna Boy's African Giant landed in 2019 and Twice as Tall won the Grammy for Best Global Music Album in 2021, Lagos wasn't celebrating a breakthrough. It was watching the world finally show up to a party it started two decades earlier.

The takeover was quiet

Wizkid's "Essence" with Tems didn't just chart — it became the song of a summer in America without softening a single thing about itself, and picked up a Grammy nomination along the way. Rema's "Calm Down," reworked with Selena Gomez, turned into one of the longest-running global smashes of the streaming era. Davido's "Fall" had already been doing quiet business for years. Ayra Starr's "Rush" made her the face of the new wave. Asake released some of the most obsessively streamed music on the planet from a base in Lagos that never pretended to care about Western validation.

Notice the pattern: nobody asked permission. The industry's discovery narrative — "we found this exciting new sound" — is a press release. The reality is a city of twenty-something million people with the densest concentration of musical talent on earth, a club scene that never sleeps, and a diaspora stretching from London to Houston that carried the sound across every border the industry failed to cross.

The alté kids rewrote it

While the mainstream ran the playbook, Lagos's underground was already drafting the sequel. The alté scene — alternative, genre-fluid, allergic to formula — bubbled up in the late 2010s with artists like Odunsi (The Engine), Cruel Santino, and an early Tems, who came out of that world before becoming the voice on everybody's favorite song. Alté is where Lagos experiments: R&B, funk, electronic music, and Afrobeats melted down and recast by kids who grew up on everything at once. If the global Afrobeats wave ever cools, Lagos won't. The lab is still open.

The industry's discovery narrative is a press release. Lagos is the factory, the lab, and the afterparty — and it was never waiting for permission.

The hype cycle is boring

Here's what actually annoys Lagos about the Afrobeats moment: the flattening. One genre label, one playlist aesthetic, one "Africa rising" narrative slapped over a city with a hundred scenes. Street-pop, alté, gospel, fuji, the New Afrika Shrine keeping Fela's flame literal — Lagos contains multitudes, and the global story only has room for the stadium acts.

But Lagos has been through hype cycles before, and it has a proven strategy: outlast them. The city will keep making the music whether the West is watching or not, because it was doing it when the West wasn't. The next Burna is already in a Surulere studio. The next Tems is already uploading. The world will "discover" them in five years and write breathless features about a scene that was never lost.

Afrobeats ate the world. Lagos, sensibly, is already thinking about dessert.

The take: the global Afrobeats boom isn't a discovery story — it's a delayed-arrival story. Lagos built the sound, the infrastructure, and the stars, then waited for the industry to catch up. It always does.
