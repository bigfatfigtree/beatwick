SCENE REPORT · TOKYO

City Pop Never Died. It Just Moved to Your Algorithm

Mariya Takeuchi's "Plastic Love" sat on YouTube for years, then conquered the world. Now Tokyo's new generation rides the same wave — on purpose.

BY THE BEATWICK DESK · OCTOBER 14, 2026 · 4 MIN READ

The strangest comeback in music history didn't happen on a stage. It happened in a recommendation column, sometime around 2017, when a four-minute Japanese pop song from 1984 started colonizing the "up next" queue of millions of people who had never heard of city pop — and then, crucially, never left.

"Plastic Love" is the song. Mariya Takeuchi recorded it in 1984 for her album Variety, and it was a hit in Japan in the way most things were hits in bubble-era Japan: big, glossy, and then filed away. Decades later, a fan upload of the track — paired with a moody, anonymous-looking sleeve photo — sat on YouTube accumulating views like sediment. Then the algorithm noticed. Vaporwave kids, lo-fi hip-hop streams, anime-adjacent internet corners: they all converged on the same silky, melancholy four minutes. By 2019 the label had to release an official music video for a 35-year-old song, because the internet had essentially re-released it themselves.

And "Plastic Love" was just the flagship. Tatsuro Yamashita's "Sparkle" and "Ride on Time." Anri's "Cat's Eye." Miki Matsubara's "Stay With Me," a 1979 single that got a proper 12-inch reissue in 2020 because demand wouldn't quit. An entire era of Japanese music — the soundtrack of neon, bullet trains, and an economic miracle — had been sitting in bargain bins, and the algorithm dug it up like an archaeologist with a taste for yacht rock.

The nostalgia was outsourced

Here's the beautiful part: most of the people who made city pop a global phenomenon don't live in Japan and don't speak Japanese. The revival was led by Western internet culture, which heard in those chrome-plated grooves something the Japanese mainstream had politely moved on from. It was nostalgia for a city most listeners had never visited, in a decade most listeners were born after, in a language most listeners don't speak. The algorithm doesn't need context. It just needs a groove.

This should have been a one-season retro craze. It wasn't, because Tokyo's new generation was watching — and unlike the algorithm, they have context. They grew up with the revival. And they started making music that treats city pop not as a museum piece but as raw material.

The new wave knows the old wave

Tokyo's current scene is quietly fluent in its own history in a way few cities manage. The indie boom — bands like Suchmos with their sleek, groove-heavy sound, King Gnu's arena-scale eclecticism, Fujii Kaze's featherlight funk-pop — carries city pop's DNA openly: the meticulous production, the yacht-rock sheen, the confidence that pop music can be sophisticated without being boring. These aren't retro acts. They're the grandkids, and the family resemblance is on purpose.

City pop was never a relic. It was a time capsule — and the algorithm just happened to be the kid who dug it up.

Meanwhile the underground keeps doing what Tokyo's underground has always done: everything at once. The shoegaze scene (Coaltar of the Deepers and their disciples) is still loud and still vital. J-rap — Kohh, ¥ellow Bucks and the new school — grew up alongside the city pop revival and samples it like a birthright. In Shimokitazawa's basements and Koenji's live houses, kids are making music that sounds like all of Tokyo's eras playing at the same time.

Tokyo outlasts its trends

Every few years the West "discovers" a Japanese scene — city pop, then Shibuya-kei reissues, then the shoegaze revival, then the vinyl-collector obsession with Japanese pressings — and every few years Tokyo absorbs the attention and gets on with it. The city has been a music capital for half a century. It doesn't need your discovery. It needed your bandwidth, and it got it.

The lesson of "Plastic Love" isn't that old songs can go viral. It's that Tokyo's musical past was always good enough to survive the future — it was just waiting for distribution. The algorithm finally provided it. And the new generation, raised on the revival, is making sure the next thing the algorithm digs up will be theirs.

Every city has a sound. Tokyo's is the sound of the past arriving right on schedule — and the future already in the queue.

The take: city pop's global conquest wasn't a revival — it was a delayed release. Tokyo's new generation grew up inside the algorithm's discovery and is now manufacturing the next one on purpose.
